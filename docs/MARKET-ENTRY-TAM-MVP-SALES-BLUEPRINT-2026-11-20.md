## 1. Executive decision

The first market to win is **loc care in Eldoret Town**. NAKEBUSEN already has a defined service direction, approved starting-price anchors and a direct enquiry route. The first MVP is therefore a dependable paid service and customer-conversion system—not a booking application.

The order of execution is:

1. **NAKEBUSEN LOCS:** acquire, serve and retain paying loc-care customers; measure service economics and repeat demand.
2. **NAKEBUSEN DIGITAL, powered by WARENA:** validate narrowly scoped technical engagements with real buyers; quote only after discovery.
3. **Internal operations capability:** use simple, controlled records to improve enquiry handling, scheduling, service history and financial visibility. Automate only after the workflow is understood.
4. **IKAI:** continue reflective-computing and mirror research. Do not forecast product revenue before a tested prototype and paying pilot exist.
5. **GENJE:** continue creative, movement and cultural practice. Test paid performances, workshops or commissions as distinct opportunities; do not count speculative audience reach as sales.
6. **FELOH Corpus:** maintain shared knowledge, evidence, governance and cross-domain consistency; it is not a checkout business.

The near-term commercial thesis is deliberately narrow: a trusted practitioner, clear service scope, transparent starting prices, direct communication, consistent delivery and a reason to return.

## 3. TAM, SAM and SOM — definitions and discipline

- **TAM — Total Addressable Market:** annual spend if every relevant customer in a clearly defined market purchased the specified service at the assumed frequency and price.
- **SAM — Serviceable Available Market:** the portion of TAM reachable with the current geography, service scope, delivery method, equipment and practitioner capacity.
- **SOM — Serviceable Obtainable Market:** the portion realistically capturable over a stated period, constrained by awareness, conversion, competition, operating hours, capacity, cash and execution.

A large industry statistic is not automatically NAKEBUSEN's TAM. National beauty and hair-care retail figures describe product sales; they are not local salon or loc-care service revenue. Population is not a customer count. Competitor listings are not market share. Social followers and likes are not paying demand.

### 3.1 LOCS bottom-up sizing

Use a service-specific model:

`TAM_{annual} = N \times F \times A`

Where:

- `N` = number of people in the defined geography who use or are likely to purchase the specific loc-care service;
- `F` = average paid visits per customer per year;
- `A` = average realised revenue per visit (not merely the advertised starting price).

Calculate separately for maintenance/retie, retouch and styling, and artificial loc installation. Do not assume all customers buy every service. Avoid double-counting people who use more than one service.

**Illustrative arithmetic only — not a measured market:** if 1,000 relevant customers each made four paid visits a year at an average realised spend of KES 1,200, the implied annual service pool would be KES 4,800,000. None of those three inputs is established by this example. Replace each with observed local evidence before describing the result as a market estimate.

### 3.2 Geography and SAM

Initial SAM is not all Kenya. It is the serviceable customer base within **Eldoret Town**, initially prioritising Karibu Mdosi and nearby areas that can be served reliably. Extend to other Uasin Gishu communities only where travel, appointment time, service delivery and travel charges make the work viable.

For every service, record:
- practical service radius and travel time;
- whether delivery is at the agreed business location, by arrangement elsewhere, or remote;
- service duration and preparation requirements;
- materials, equipment and supply constraints;
- whether the service can be delivered safely and consistently;
- the customer's likely repeat interval.

The county or city population may contextualise the geography but must never be substituted for the number of qualified loc-care buyers.

### 3.3 Capacity-constrained SOM

For a service business, obtainable revenue is bounded by capacity:

`SOM_{annual} = C \times D \times W \times R \times 12`

Where:
- `C` = sustainable appointments per working day;
- `D` = working days per week;
- `W` = realistic utilisation (0–1), accounting for gaps, cancellations, administration and rest;
- `R` = average realised revenue per completed appointment.

This is a capacity ceiling, not a forecast. Calculate each service's duration and mix separately. Do not assume every available slot sells or that maximum utilisation is sustainable. A realistic SOM must also be constrained by qualified enquiries, conversion, retention, local demand and customer acquisition.

### 3.4 DIGITAL / WARENA sizing

Do not estimate WARENA's market as the entire Kenyan ICT industry. Build a bottom-up model from identifiable buyer groups and specific problems:

- individuals and small teams needing GNU/Linux installation, troubleshooting or backup readiness;
- small organisations dependent on workstations, networks and shared systems;
- learning spaces, community infrastructure and independent operators needing scoped technical support;
- learners paying for practical coaching.

For each offer:

`TAM = number\ of\ qualified\ buyers \times annual\ engagements\ per\ buyer \times average\ realised\ fee`

Use interviews, actual quotations, paid pilots and repeat engagements to replace assumptions. Separate one-off installation work from recurring support agreements. Do not promise 24/7 support, enterprise uptime, cybersecurity certification or guaranteed recovery without the capability and evidence to deliver it.

### 3.5 IKAI and GENJE market sizing

IKAI's future market, if any, must be sized around a validated use case and identified buyer—such as independent practitioners, salons, training providers or research partners—not the global beauty-tech or XR market. First prove that a specific user problem is important, that a prototype addresses it safely, and that a buyer will pay.

GENJE opportunities should be sized by distinct paid formats: performance bookings, commissions, workshops, collaborations or licensing. An audience estimate is not equivalent to a booking pipeline. Price each format against preparation, rehearsal, equipment, travel, rights and delivery costs.

## 4. MVP definition and release order

An MVP is the smallest offer that can be delivered to a real customer, paid for, evaluated and improved. It does not have to be software.

### MVP 1 — NAKEBUSEN LOCS (launch and validate first)

**Offer:** reliable loc-care services with clear starting prices, direct consultation, agreed scope, confirmed availability, quality delivery and follow-up.

Current public starting-price anchors:
- Retouch & Styling — **KES 700+**
- Sisterlocs Retie — **KES 1,500+**
- Artificial Locs Installation — **KES 3,500+**

These are starting prices, not guaranteed final quotes. Final price depends on service scope, loc condition, materials and time. No braiding or shaving is included in the current public offer. An enquiry does not confirm a booking; availability and final terms are agreed directly.

**Minimum customer journey:**
1. Customer discovers the service through the website, referral, local discovery or an approved campaign.
2. Customer sees what the service is, the starting price and the contact route.
3. Customer starts a WhatsApp or telephone enquiry.
4. Practitioner understands the requested service, current loc condition and desired outcome.
5. Scope, any reference image, likely duration, final quote and availability are discussed.
6. Customer and practitioner agree the appointment and terms directly.
7. Service is delivered and checked.
8. Payment and direct costs are recorded in the controlled business record.
9. Customer receives relevant aftercare guidance and, with consent, a follow-up / maintenance reminder.
10. Repeat booking or referral is measured.

**MVP acceptance criteria:**
- A customer can understand the offer and starting prices without decoding jargon.
- The customer can initiate a direct conversation easily.
- The practitioner can qualify the request and explain scope and price.
- Availability and terms are explicitly agreed before the appointment is treated as confirmed.
- Delivery is repeatable and the customer can assess the result.
- Revenue, direct costs, time and feedback are recorded.
- Customer records and images are handled with consent and are not placed in public repositories.

The existing static website and WhatsApp route are sufficient to validate this MVP. Do not build a custom booking platform, CRM, payment API, automated STK push or automated booking-success state as a prerequisite.

### MVP 2 — NAKEBUSEN DIGITAL / WARENA

Start with one or two bounded offers selected from real buyer problems, not a broad promise to solve every IT issue. Candidate pilots:

| Pilot offer | Deliverable | Commercial method |
|---|---|---|
| Linux Setup / Recovery | Agreed setup or troubleshooting task plus handover notes | Bounded diagnostic, then scoped quote |
| Technical Troubleshooting | Time-boxed investigation of one defined issue | Session fee once duration and limits are known |
| Small-System Setup | Installation/configuration of one specified service | Fixed scope, exclusions and acceptance checks |
| Backup Readiness Review | Review and written recommendations; restore test only if separately authorised | Fixed assessment scope |
| Linux / Computing Coaching | Practical session around a learner's task | Per session or workshop |

Before work begins, agree problem statement, equipment and versions, scope, exclusions, backup/rollback approach, downtime, deliverables, verification, fee, travel/material costs and support boundary. No public price is established merely by listing these candidates. All customer engagements transact through NAKEBUSEN.

### MVP 3 — internal operations system

Use the simplest controlled tool that supports the workflow. Begin with a private ledger or local-first record system; do not publish customer information in GitHub.

Minimum fields:
- pseudonymous customer/reference ID;
- enquiry date and source;
- requested service;
- quoted amount and agreed amount;
- status: enquiry / qualified / quoted / confirmed / completed / cancelled / lost;
- appointment and actual service duration;
- direct materials and travel costs;
- consent status for photos and follow-up;
- completion notes, feedback and next recommended maintenance window.

Keep contact details and any identifiable customer data in a restricted operational location, separate from analytics and public repositories. Collect only what is needed. Define access, retention, backup and deletion practices before expanding the record system.

### Later products — gated, not assumed

- IKAI Crown Record / mirror: research and prototype validation first.
- Salon operations software: build only after NAKEBUSEN has a measured internal workflow and at least several independent practitioners confirm the same problem and willingness to pay.
- GENJE movement / creative systems: prototype or commission only against a defined creative/research brief and agreed rights.
- Hardware: require technical feasibility, safety, maintainability, supplier/manufacturing economics and paid pilot evidence.

### Enquiry qualification

Ask only what is needed to understand the request:
- which service is sought;
- current loc condition and desired outcome;
- relevant length/density/material information where needed;
- optional reference image, only when useful and voluntarily supplied;
- preferred date/time;
- location or travel needs, where relevant.

Then explain the likely scope, quote and availability. Do not imply that sending a message, choosing a preferred date or receiving an automated acknowledgement confirms a booking.

### Sales discipline

- Record the source of each qualified enquiry.
- Record requests that cannot be served and why.
- Record quoted versus realised price.
- Follow up only where appropriate and with consent.
- Do not use blanket discounting as the default conversion strategy.
- Test one change at a time: service wording, price visibility, portfolio evidence, CTA placement or follow-up timing.
- Treat customer trust, respectful service and consistent delivery as the first defensible advantage.

## 6. Pricing and unit economics

Starting prices are entry points, not a complete rate card. For each service, measure:

- practitioner time, including preparation and cleanup;
- materials and consumables;
- travel and delivery costs, if any;
- rework, wastage and cancellations;
- allocated operating overhead;
- actual price realised;
- customer acquisition effort;
- capacity displaced by the appointment.

Core calculations:

`Gross\ revenue = completed\ paid\ appointments \times average\ realised\ transaction`

`Contribution = revenue - direct\ variable\ costs`

`Contribution\ per\ service\ hour = contribution / total\ practitioner\ hours`

`CAC = attributable\ sales\ and\ marketing\ cost / new\ paying\ customers`

`Revenue\ LTV = average\ realised\ transaction \times annual\ visits \times retained\ years`

For decisions, prefer contribution LTV over revenue LTV. Do not call contribution profit: overhead, taxes, owner compensation, depreciation, financing and other operating costs remain to be accounted for.

An illustrative calculator can help test assumptions, but its output is not a forecast. For example, 20 working days × 3 appointments per day × KES 1,400 average transaction gives KES 84,000 gross monthly revenue before costs. It assumes all 60 slots are completed and paid; it excludes cancellations, capacity variation, overhead and owner compensation. Never publish this as actual or expected revenue without evidence.

Maintain separate financial views:
1. NAKEBUSEN LOCS;
2. NAKEBUSEN DIGITAL engagements;
3. WARENA technical research and infrastructure costs;
4. IKAI and GENJE experimental/research costs.

Do not hide experimental R&D expenditure inside loc-service margins.

## 7. First 90 days — validation cadence

These are proposed operating phases and internal learning targets, not external benchmarks or guaranteed outcomes.

### Days 1–15 — establish baseline
- Reconcile public service names, starting prices, contact details and enquiry caveat against the approved catalogue.
- Document scope and quote factors for each loc service.
- Start a private enquiry, booking, delivery and cost ledger.
- Record actual appointment duration and materials.
- Audit the website-to-WhatsApp journey on mobile.
- Define one bounded DIGITAL pilot only if a real buyer problem is available.

### Days 16–30 — acquire and observe
- Publish service-specific information and genuine consented work examples.
- Ask satisfied customers for referrals without pressure.
- Record enquiry source, requested service, quote and conversion.
- Interview at least five prospective DIGITAL buyers about their last real technical problem, how they resolved it and what it cost.
- Log lost leads and objections rather than guessing why people did not book.

### Days 31–60 — improve conversion and retention
- Compare enquiry-to-booking conversion by service and channel.
- Measure actual service-hour contribution.
- Follow up with consenting customers and track repeat demand.
- Test one copy, CTA or process change at a time.
- Deliver a bounded DIGITAL pilot only after written scope and acceptance criteria.

### Days 61–90 — decide from evidence
- Identify which services generate repeat demand and positive contribution.
- Identify bottlenecks: practitioner hours, materials, travel, conversion or customer acquisition.
- Review cancellations, rework, feedback and referral sources.
- Decide whether any internal software need is proven by repeated operational friction.
- Continue, revise or stop offers that do not demonstrate demand or viable economics.

### Suggested internal learning targets

For an initial 90-day validation window, a working target range could be 30–50 qualified enquiries, 15–30 paid appointments, 5–10 repeat customers, 3–5 referrals and at least 10 feedback records. These are planning targets only; they are not market benchmarks, promised results or proof of product-market fit. Record the actual baseline before evaluating performance.

## 9. Customer discovery and evidence plan

Existing research is a useful hypothesis register, not a substitute for primary customer evidence. A practical exploratory sample—not a statistically representative survey—could include:

- 20–30 prospective loc-care customers;
- 10–15 existing or recent beauty customers;
- 5–10 locticians / beauty practitioners;
- 5 salon owners or managers;
- 3–5 technical or research partners for relevant future tracks.

Ask about recent behaviour rather than abstract enthusiasm:
- When did they last pay for the service?
- What exactly did they buy and what did they pay?
- How often do they return?
- What was frustrating about the last experience?
- What alternatives did they consider?
- What would make them switch or stay?
- What evidence would justify a higher price?
- For B2B buyers: what system problem occurred, what did downtime cost, who approved the spend and what solution was purchased?

Separate stated interest from deposits, paid pilots and repeat purchases. Record date, geography, sample limitations and the exact question asked. Do not present a convenience sample as a population estimate.

## 10. Investment gates

No later-stage investment should be treated as automatic.

1. **Demand gate:** do identifiable customers pay for the service repeatedly?
2. **Economic gate:** does the service produce sustainable contribution after direct costs and practitioner time?
3. **Operational gate:** can the work be delivered consistently without unsafe workload or quality decline?
4. **Workflow gate:** is there repeated, documented operational friction that a tool would solve?
5. **Product gate:** will independent buyers pay for the proposed software, device or research output?
6. **Scale gate:** can quality, support, privacy, supply and cash flow withstand a larger customer base?

If a gate fails, revise the offer or stop the investment. Do not use the existence of a prototype, repository or research document as evidence that customers want a product.

| Risk | Control |

| Inflated TAM from national retail or population data | Use bottom-up service-specific customers, frequency and realised spend |
| Confusing interest with demand | Prioritise completed paid transactions and repeat behaviour |
| Underpricing practitioner time | Measure service duration, direct costs and contribution per hour |
| Overbooking or inconsistent quality | Capacity-based scheduling and realistic utilisation |
| Premature custom software | Operate the manual workflow first; automate repeated, measured friction |
| Research presented as a product | Explicit maturity labels and evidence gates |
